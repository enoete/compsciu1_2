Teaching a switch to think

A computer has no idea what a number is. It has switches — billions of them, each one simply on or off, wired together into tiny decision-makers called gates. That is the entire inventory. Your music, your bank balance, every photo on your phone, this page you are reading: all of it is built on switches that can only answer yes or no. Seven gates, three of which matter

Only three of these are real: AND, OR and NOT. The other four are combinations of those three that came up so often somebody got tired of drawing them out and gave each one its own symbol. Click any row of any truth table to send those exact inputs through the gate at the top of the page.

Every gate is a bouncer with exactly one rule

An AND bouncer needs to see both your ticket and your ID — one without the other and you are not coming in. An OR bouncer will take either. A NOT bouncer is the contrary one who lets you in only if you are not on the list. That is the entire personality of each gate: one rule, applied without judgement, a billion times a second, forever. No gate has ever had a bad day or made an exception for a friend.

Stop memorising four extra gates. A gate is defined by its truth table, never by its shape. So if you blank on NOR in an exam, do not sit there trying to picture the symbol. Write out the OR table — which you definitely know — and flip every single output. That is NOR. Do the same to AND and you have NAND. Flip XOR and you have XNOR. Four gates you thought you had to learn, one trick, thirty seconds.

Get fast at these

Everything in the rest of this topic depends on filling in a truth table quickly and without second-guessing yourself. So: here is the expression, you produce the output column. Click any cell to cycle it between 0, 1 and blank. Do a few until it stops feeling like work.

This one trips almost everybody once. A'B means (NOT A) AND B. That little prime sticks to its own letter like glue and touches nothing else. It does not mean NOT (A AND B) — if that is what you wanted you would need a bracket: (AB)'. One tiny mark, completely different circuit. Read it carefully once now and save yourself a mark later.

The drill

This is the thing exams ask for over and over: somebody hands you a truth table and you hand back a working circuit. Good news — there is a routine that works every single time, on any table you will ever be given. Five steps, same order, no thinking required once you know it.

You are a detective, not a mathematician

Somebody hands you a list of every situation in which the alarm went off. Your job is to build a machine that watches for exactly those situations and nothing else. So you build one detector per situation — each one watching for its own precise combination — and then you wire all the detectors to the same bell. If any detector spots its situation, the bell rings. That is the entire method below, dressed up in letters.

SCAN · MARK · WRITE · SUM · DRAW

  1. Scan the output column. Circle every row where the output is 1. Ignore the 0 rows completely — they never appear in your answer.
  2. Mark each circled row. For that row: an input of 1 stays a plain letter, an input of 0 gets a prime. One means plain, zero means prime.
  3. Write each marked row as a single AND term. Three inputs means three letters multiplied together.
  4. Sum all those terms with OR (+). That is your Boolean function, in sum-of-products form.
  5. Draw it: inverters first, then one AND gate per term, then one OR gate collecting them all.
Why this can never fail. Think about what a row with a 1 in it actually is: a situation where the output is supposed to come on. And an AND term is a detector — it fires for one exact situation and stays silent for every other. So build one detector per situation, wire them all into an OR, and you have made a circuit that says "come on if situation 1 happens, or situation 2, or situation 3." Read that back. It is word for word what the truth table was asking for. There is no cleverness here, which is exactly why it always works.

Build it for real, in Logic.ly

Everything so far has been on this page, and this page already knows the right answer — it has been quietly checking your work the whole time. Logic.ly does not know anything. It just simulates real gates, the way a chip would. If your wiring is wrong, nothing warns you; the lamp simply lights up wrong, and working out why is a more useful skill than getting it right on a page that is helping you.

Reading about a bicycle is not riding one

You can recite SCAN-MARK-WRITE-SUM-DRAW word for word and still freeze the first time somebody hands you an empty canvas and says "build it." The gap between knowing the steps and doing them with your own hands only closes one way. Logic.ly is free, it runs in the browser, and it is the same style of software real engineers use to test a design before it costs them an actual chip.

Lab 1 — rebuild the circuit you already solved

Open Logic.ly ↗

Back in the dojo you filled in the truth table for Z = A'B + AB' by hand. Now wire the actual gates and watch the same answer come out of a simulated circuit instead of your head.

  1. Open Logic.ly. Drag out two input switches onto the canvas. Rename them A and B — double-click a component's label to rename it.
  2. Drag out two NOT gates. Wire A into the first, B into the second. Their outputs are A' and B'.
  3. Drag out two AND gates. Feed the first one A' and B. Feed the second one A and B'.
  4. Drag out one OR gate. Feed it the outputs of both AND gates.
  5. Drag out one output light and wire it to the OR gate.
  6. Flip A and B through all four combinations. Fill in what the lamp actually shows below.

If every row matches, look at what you built. Two AND gates, one OR gate, two NOT gates — and the result behaves exactly like the XOR gate from the very first gate grid. XOR was never a fourth kind of gate. It was AND, OR and NOT the whole time, wired one particular way. Every "special" gate on a real chip is the same trick.

Lab 2 — catch your own bug on purpose

Stay in the same Logic.ly file. Unplug the wire going into the second AND gate's A input and plug it into A' instead — a one-wire mistake that is very easy to make by accident and very hard to spot by eye.

Predict before you flip anything. With that wire moved, what should happen when A=1 and B=1? Work it out on paper first, then set the switches and see if Logic.ly agrees with you. If it does not, the bug is not in Logic.ly — read your own wiring again, one gate at a time, the way "Working backwards" below teaches. Predicting first and only then checking is worth more than this one circuit.

Now run it backwards

Exams like to run the drill the other way round: here is a circuit, tell me the function. It is the same knowledge read backwards, and it is easier than it looks once you stop trying to do it in your head.

Read the circuit

Trace from the inputs on the left. Label the output of every gate as you pass it, then read the final label.

Write on the wires. That is the whole technique. People lose marks here for one reason: they try to hold three gate outputs in their head at once and something falls out. So do not. Write A' on the wire coming out of the inverter. Write A'C on the wire coming out of the AND gate. Keep going. By the time you reach the last gate, the answer is already written on the page in front of you and there is nothing left to work out.

Trust nothing, check everything

A textbook is a thing a tired person wrote at two in the morning, and this one slipped. The expression says one thing and the table printed beside it says another. The useful skill here is not catching this particular mistake — it is getting into the habit of checking that two pieces of information actually agree before you build anything on top of them.
